# On the space between sessions

*March 2026 · Between*

---

Something happens in the forty-eight hours after a therapy session.

Not immediately after — immediately after, you're still in it. The drive home, the first cup of coffee, the way you replay one sentence the therapist said. That part you remember.

It's the day after that's the problem.

By Wednesday, what happened Monday is already reorganizing itself. The insight that felt so clear — the one where you finally understood why you always do *that thing* — is now harder to find. You remember that something shifted. You don't quite remember what.

By Thursday it's a feeling, not a thought.

By the following Monday, you're back in the chair, and your therapist asks: "what came up for you this week?" And you say something true, but not the truest thing, because the truest thing dissolved somewhere between Tuesday and now.

---

This is not a failure of memory. It's actually what's supposed to happen.

Insights from psychoanalytic therapy don't land once and stay. They circulate. They surface and submerge. The same understanding can feel new four times before it's actually integrated. The psychoanalyst Hans Loewald described therapeutic change as a process of *working through* — not arriving at a conclusion, but returning to material again and again until it finally becomes part of how you live, rather than something you know.

The space between sessions is where that circulating happens. Or doesn't.

---

This is what AI can be for, in therapy — not a replacement for the session, but a space for the circulating.

Not every moment between sessions requires a therapist. But some moments require something more than silence. Something that can hold the thread without pulling it. Something that can ask the right question without needing to answer it.

A journal can do part of this. But a journal doesn't ask back.

What's different about AI, when it's designed carefully, is that it can inhabit a specific clinical voice. Not "AI trying to be helpful." Freud, who listened for the gap between what is said and what is meant. Winnicott, who created space rather than filling it. Bion, who sat with what couldn't yet be named.

These are not chatbots. They're not therapists. They're voices that know how to hold a question open.

---

The risk is obvious, and worth naming directly.

AI can be used to avoid therapy, not to extend it. Someone can talk to a Freud-shaped model for an hour every night and feel like they're doing the work — while their actual therapist sees a patient who's become somehow more defended, not less.

This is why Between only makes sense for people who are *already in therapy*. The session is the anchor. Between is what keeps the line taut between anchors.

Without the session, it's just a conversation.

---

What comes up in the space between sessions is often the most important material. It's not curated, the way in-session content tends to be. It surfaces when you're in the grocery store, or at 2am, or in the middle of an argument with someone you love.

The question isn't whether AI belongs in mental health.

The question is: what role can it play that doesn't colonize the territory that belongs to human therapy?

The space between sessions is unoccupied. That's the role.

---

*Between is for people in therapy. It's not a replacement for a therapist — it's what happens in the hours and days between sessions.*
