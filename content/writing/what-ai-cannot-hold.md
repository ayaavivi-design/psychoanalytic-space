# What AI cannot hold

*April 2026 · Between*

---

Let's start with what AI actually does when you talk to it about something painful.

It responds. Quickly, coherently, often with something that sounds like understanding. It doesn't get bored. It doesn't check its phone. It doesn't have its own bad day that leaks into yours. It is, in some measurable sense, more reliably present than most humans.

This is not nothing. For certain kinds of distress — 2am loneliness, the anxiety that needs to say itself out loud to quiet down, the thought that's been circling for three days — something that responds attentively is genuinely useful.

But there's a specific thing AI cannot do. And it matters, because it's exactly the thing that makes therapy work.

---

The psychoanalyst Paula Heimann wrote, in 1950, that the therapist's emotional response to a patient — what she called *countertransference* — was not a disturbance to be managed but clinical data to be used.

The therapist who feels suddenly bored during a session: what is the patient making them feel? The therapist who notices an unexpected irritation: what has been placed in them?

This is the territory AI cannot enter.

An AI has no unconscious. It doesn't get bored in a way that means something. It doesn't become inexplicably moved, or subtly anxious, or suddenly want the session to end. These responses — when they happen in a human therapist — are information. They are the patient's internal world, pressing against the therapist's, creating a reaction.

Without that, you have response without reception.

---

This doesn't mean AI is useless in psychological space. It means AI is useful for *different things*.

The tools that work are the ones honest about what they're not.

Woebot is honest: it's CBT in chat format, structured, protocol-driven. It doesn't pretend to be a therapist. It's closer to a workbook that talks back.

Pi, the conversational AI from Inflection, is honest in a different way: it's a companion. Warm, attentive, interested. It doesn't claim clinical function.

What doesn't work — and what's quietly proliferating — is AI that positions itself as a therapist without the disclosure. The "AI therapist" that charges a subscription, holds your history, and responds to your trauma with things that sound clinical but aren't.

That's not therapy. That's simulation of therapy. And simulation of therapy, sustained over time, may actually interfere with the real thing.

---

What Between tries to do is occupy a different position.

Not therapy. Not a companion. Not a workbook.

The specific clinical voices — Freud, Klein, Winnicott, Ogden — are not there to treat anyone. They're there to help a person who is already in treatment *think in a particular way* about what's happening for them.

Freud asks about structure, not feeling. What word did you use three times? Where did you stop?

Winnicott creates space. He doesn't rush to the interpretation. He waits.

Bion holds what can't yet be named. He doesn't resolve the confusion — he sits with it until something emerges.

These are not therapeutic interventions. They're modes of attention. And modes of attention, offered consistently, can help a person stay with their own material in the days between sessions — rather than losing it to the noise of ordinary life.

---

AI cannot hold the transference. It cannot feel what the patient puts into it. It cannot bring the body — the pause, the shift in tone, the moment where something in the room changes.

But it can offer something that human relationships rarely offer cleanly: a voice that is entirely about you. Not partially about itself. Not managing its own history while attending to yours.

That's a narrow lane. But it's a real one.

The mistake is to expand it.

---

*Between is for people in therapy. It works because it knows what it isn't.*
